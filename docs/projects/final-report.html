Final Report: [Obesity]

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a major public health problem. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported the prevalence rate of obesity in the United States as 40.3% during 2021 - 2023 period [1]. Unlike acute illnesses, the health consequences of obesity typically develop gradually. Obesity accelerates biological aging by promoting chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction, all of which contribute to the earlier onset of age-related diseases [2]. It increases the risk of numerous chronic conditions, including type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and certain types of cancers [3, 4]. In this project I want to test the relationship between lifestyle factors and obesity. I expect for example that regular physical activity is associated with lower obesity levels. Hypothesis (H1): Healthier lifestyle habits are associated with lower obesity rates. Null hypothesis (H0): There is no correlation between lifestyle and obesity. The findings may contribute to a better understanding of the problem and alter public health strategies.

Methods

Obesity Prediction dataset was used in this project. It is available on Kaggle: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ruchikakumbhar/obesity-prediction

The dataset included 2,111 participants and 17 variables related to demographics, lifestyle, and obesity level.

RESULTS

The dataset included 2,111 participants distributed across seven weight categories. The largest group was Obesity Type I (351 participants), while the smallest group was Insufficient Weight (272 participants).

The Chi-square test showed a significant association between family history and obesity level (χ² = 621.98, p < 0.001). Participants with a family history of obesity were much more common in the higher obesity categories.

Physical activity was not normally distributed, and the assumption of equal variances was also violated. Therefore, non-parametric methods were used for the analysis. The Kruskal-Wallis test found a significant difference in physical activity across obesity levels (χ² = 88.42, p < 0.001). People with Insufficient Weight and Normal Weight had the highest average physical activity, while participants with Obesity Type III had the lowest average physical activity. Several pairwise comparisons were statistically significant according to the Games-Howell test. When physical activity was compared by gender, males generally reported higher average physical activity than females within the same obesity category. However, some obesity categories contained very few participants of one gender, so those results should be interpreted with caution.

The heat map showed that mean physical activity generally decreased as obesity level increased, especially among participants with a family history of obesity.

The scatter plots suggested a weak relationship between age and physical activity in most obesity groups. The strongest decrease in physical activity with age was observed among participants with Obesity Type III.

The Chi-square test also found a significant association between obesity and frequent consumption of high-calorie foods (χ² = 233.34, p < 0.001). Frequent high-calorie food intake was much more common among participants in the obesity categories.

Discussion

The results support my hypothesis that lifestyle factors are associated with obesity. Participants with higher obesity levels generally reported lower physical activity and were more likely to have a family history of obesity. Frequent consumption of high-calorie foods was also strongly associated with obesity.

Conclusions

This study found significant relationships between obesity and lifestyle factors. Participants with higher obesity levels generally had lower physical activity, were more likely to report a family history of obesity, and more frequently consumed high-calorie foods. The results suggest that obesity is influenced by several factors rather than only one behavior. Limitation of this study is that the dataset is observational, so it cannot prove cause and effect. In addition, some obesity groups contained very few participants of one gender, making those comparisons less reliable.
